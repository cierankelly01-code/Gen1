Pressing or laser cut and fold?

The short version: laser cut and fold while volumes are low or the design is changing; press once volumes are steady and high enough to pay back a tool.

In short: Laser cutting and folding needs no tooling, so it's usually cheaper for prototypes and lower volumes. Pressing needs a production tool, but makes a part in one or a few strokes, so it gives the lowest price per part once volumes are steady and high, typically several thousand a year or more. Near the break point, ask for both prices.

02

When does a press tool pay back?

A press tool is a one-off cost. It pays back when the saving on every part, multiplied by the number of parts you'll buy, is bigger than the tool cost. Three things move the break point:

  • Volume and life: how many parts a year, for how many years.
  • Complexity: more bends and features cost more on a press brake, which favours pressing sooner.
  • Design stability: if the design may still change, stay on laser cut and fold until it's frozen.

03

Many parts use both, one after the other

A common route: prototypes and early batches laser cut and folded while the design settles, then the part moves to presswork once volumes justify the tool. Designing with that move in mind from the start makes it painless. See our design guide for the design rules that suit both.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Is pressing always cheaper?

No. Pressing has the lowest price per part, but the production tool has to be paid for. At low volumes, laser cutting and folding is usually cheaper overall.

Can a part start on laser and move to pressing later?

Yes. It's a common route: laser cut and fold while the design settles, then a press tool once volumes are steady.

How do I find the break point for my part?
