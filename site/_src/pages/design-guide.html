01

Choosing a route

Short answer: laser cut and fold for prototypes and lower volumes, pressing once volumes reach several thousand a year and the design is settled, and fabrication when the part is thick, large or made from several pieces. Near the break point, we price both.

RouteBest forTrade-off
Laser cut & foldPrototypes, design changes, low to medium volumes, parts up to 6 mm and 3 mHigher price per part at volume
PressingRepeat volumes of settled designs, up to 5 mm, with pierced, formed and inserted features in the pressNeeds a production tool before the first part
FabricationThick, large or multi-piece parts; frames, enclosures and structuresMore labour per part; weld distortion to control
MachiningTight-tolerance features, thick sections, complex 3D shapesSlower per part than forming

Many parts change route over their life: laser cut and folded while the design settles, then moved to presswork once volumes justify a tool. Designing with that in mind from the start makes the move painless.

Design rules for pressed and folded parts

Starting guidelines, where t = material thickness
FeatureGuideline
Minimum hole diameter≥ 1 t (larger for high-strength steels)
Hole to edge≥ 1.5 t, 2 t preferred
Hole to bend≥ 2 t + inside bend radius
Inside bend radius≥ 1 t for mild steel; more for stainless, aluminium and high-strength grades
Minimum flange length≥ 4 t
Bend reliefWidth ≥ t, depth ≥ bend radius + t
Burr directionMark the burr side on the drawing if it matters for fit or handling

These are starting points, not limits. Tell us what a feature has to do and we'll tell you what's achievable.

03

Hemmed edges

A hemmed edge folds the sheet back on itself to close the edge. It does three jobs at once:

  • Safety: no sharp edge for operators, your assembly line or the end user.
  • Strength: doubling the material stiffens the panel, so you can often keep a thinner gauge.
  • Finish: a tidy closed edge that looks like a finished product, with no deburring or linishing.

Our hemming tool closes the edge in a single hit, in mild steel, stainless and aluminium, from one-off prototypes to repeat batches. If you're designing around a sharp edge, or paying someone to grind one off, it's worth a conversation.

04

Choosing a material

MaterialUse it forWatch out for
Mild steelGeneral brackets, panels and frames; good formability, lowest costNeeds a coating against corrosion
Coated steel (galvanised, pre-painted)Outdoor and construction partsCut edges and welds may need touching up
Stainless steelCorrosion resistance, food, catering and medical, cosmetic partsMore springback; specify the surface finish
AluminiumWeight-critical partsThe grade and temper decide how tight it can bend

Not sure which grade? Tell us what the part does and where it lives, and we'll suggest one. If you'd accept an equivalent grade, say so on the RFQ to avoid a re-quote later.

05

Designing for assembly

  • Inserts instead of loose fasteners: PEM nuts, studs and weld nuts fitted by us mean fewer parts on your line.
  • Choose the weld process with us: robotic MIG suits repeatable volume work, TIG suits cosmetic stainless, and spot and projection welding suit sheet assemblies.
  • One part number: if we assemble, finish and pack the product, you buy, inspect and receive one thing instead of many.

06

What to send with an RFQ

  • A 3D model (STEP) and a dimensioned drawing (PDF or DXF)
  • Material and thickness, and any equivalents you'd accept
  • Quantity per order and per year
  • Finish or coating, and any cosmetic faces
  • Critical tolerances and how they're measured
  • Approval needed (first-article report or PPAP level), packaging and delivery location
  • When you need the first parts
